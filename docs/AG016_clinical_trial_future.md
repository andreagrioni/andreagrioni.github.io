# Who Will Run the Clinical Trial of the Future?

*By Andrea Grioni*

<mark > UPDATE 20261111:</mark> Added links to related articles on this blog.

---

A clinical trial site has a question. It travels to the Clinical Research Associate in the country team, then on to the global trial team. Sometimes the answer is immediate. Other times someone has to dig through study documentation to find it.

Now imagine the site or CRA asking an AI system that works over a controlled, current set of trial documents. It wouldn’t just answer. It would point to the relevant documents, the precise passages and their context. I first sketched this idea in [Learning to Think Like a Clinical Development Professional](story.html?file=docs/AG014_clinical_development.md), and it has stayed with me since.

The site query doesn’t disappear, but who answers it, how fast, and with what skills may all change. After BioTechX, Festival of Biologics and the TransPerfect Life Sciences C3 Summit in Basel, that is the question I keep returning to: how will AI change the way clinical trials are run, and who will run them?

The three events covered different ground. BioTechX focused on AI and data, Festival of Biologics on how these technologies interact, and the C3 Summit on clinical content, inspection readiness, the Trial Master File and AI’s role in accessing information. The conversations kept converging on the same point.

## Attention, not replacement

Clinical trials don’t suffer from an information shortage. Clinical data, safety metrics, study documents and operational history all compete for human attention. The most useful framing I’ve found is to see AI not as a replacement for the clinical expert, but as an attention-allocation technology: it helps the expert decide where to look.

There’s an amusing parallel with [*Attention Is All You Need*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762) (Vaswani et al., 2017), the paper that introduced the Transformer. In clinical development, attention is certainly not all we need. We still require context, traceability, expertise and judgement. But directing scarce attention to the right information is a powerful place to start.

## Who does the work

In the site-query example, routine questions get resolved closer to where they originate, while global teams focus on escalations, interpretation and strategic judgement. I think of this as a decentralisation of trial knowledge: not decentralised trials in the conventional sense, but decentralised access to the knowledge needed to run them.

The same applies to inspections. When health authorities ask why something happened, finding an answer helps, but the real requirement is retrieving the supporting evidence and context quickly.

This only works if answers are consistent across sites, based on current document versions, and traceable. Without that, we would just be moving the problem.

## Who needs which skills

If information becomes easier to retrieve and interrogate, knowing where it lives matters less. Knowing how to ask the right question matters more. My line manager regularly reminds me that this is one of the most important parts of clinical development, and the more I work with AI, the more right that advice seems.

AI may become remarkably good at finding, connecting and analysing information. Someone still has to recognise which question is worth asking, challenge the answer, spot missing context and decide what happens next. Tomorrow’s clinical-development professional may need clinical understanding, trial methodology, data literacy, AI literacy and critical judgement. Not everyone needs to become a machine-learning engineer, but understanding what these systems can do, how they fail and when to challenge them may become ordinary clinical literacy.

There is a catch, though. Judgement is largely built by doing the routine work: digging through documents, answering the site query, noticing what doesn’t fit. If AI takes over that work, how will the next generation build the judgement we say matters most? We may need to design that learning deliberately rather than expect it to happen by accident.

## Where this is heading

I believe the AI-enabled trial will become real, and increasingly so. Most of the manual, repetitive work (answering routine queries, checking documents, reconciling data, preparing reports) will run in the background, with people stepping in where judgement is needed.

That makes the question of who runs these trials more pressing, not less. The people who run them will spend less time doing the work and more time deciding what to ask, what to trust and what to escalate.

Which raises a question we are not asking enough. We talk a lot about keeping a “human in the loop”. We talk far less about who that human is, what they have been trained to judge, and what they can realistically check. I’ll take that on in a follow-up article.
